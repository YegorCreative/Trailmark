# Kobuk Valley sources

Live pages used:

- Park overview / index: https://www.nps.gov/kova/index.htm
  - Great Kobuk Sand Dunes described as rising unexpectedly out of the tundra and trees. Caribou migrate north in summer, south in fall, crossing the dunes. Onion Portage (Paatitaaq) named as a caribou-harvesting site for about 9,000 years. Kobuk River called an ancient and current corridor for people and wildlife. Headquarters at the Northwest Arctic Heritage Center, Kotzebue.
- History and culture: https://www.nps.gov/kova/learn/historyculture/index.htm
  - Iñupiat continue hunting caribou at Onion Portage "as their ancestors have done for ten thousand years." Kobuk Valley "home to Arctic civilization since the last Ice Age." Traditions along the Kobuk River span "over 8,000 years." J. Louis Giddings named as a pivotal figure in Arctic archaeology at the site.
- Basic information: https://www.nps.gov/kova/planyourvisit/basicinfo.htm
  - No roads or maintained trails; access by small plane or river; visitors must be self-sufficient.
- WebSearch of nps.gov sources (Geodiversity Atlas / im/arcn): acreage 708,489 hectares / 1,750,716 acres. Located about 35 miles (56 km) north of the Arctic Circle in Northwest Arctic Borough, sharing a boundary with Noatak National Preserve. Established as a national monument December 1, 1978; redesignated a national park December 2, 1980. Three dune fields named: Great Kobuk Sand Dunes, Hunt River Sand Dunes, Little Kobuk River Sand Dunes.

Conflicting figures: Onion Portage occupation is given three ways across NPS pages — about 9,000 years (overview), "ten thousand years" (history and culture), and "over 8,000 years" (same page, subsistence traditions). No statistics page settles it. Used "more than 8,000 years," the floor every figure supports.

No dune-height figure is stated on any page opened for this essay, so none is given.

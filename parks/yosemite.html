About Yosemite

Yosemite National Park sits in the western Sierra Nevada of California, carved over millions of years by glaciers that sculpted its sheer granite walls and broad valley floor. President Lincoln first set aside Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Grove in 1864 — a remarkably farsighted act that predated the National Park Service by over half a century. Full national park protection followed in 1890, driven in large part by John Muir’s passionate advocacy.

The park is defined by its concentration of granite landmarks. El Capitan rises more than 3,000 feet from the valley floor in a single sheer face, drawing climbers from every continent. Half Dome’s distinctive silhouette has become one of the enduring icons of American wilderness. Yosemite Falls drops 2,425 feet — among the tallest waterfalls in North America — roaring each spring as snowmelt pours off the high country.

Beyond the famous valley, Yosemite holds ancient groves of giant sequoia, the vast open expanse of Tuolumne Meadows, and over 800 miles of trails reaching well above 13,000 feet. Black bears, mule deer, peregrine falcons, and more than 400 other species share the landscape with roughly 3.5 million visitors each year.

Park Overview

A four-square-mile sequoia grove in 1890, a Sierra canyon park in 1940

General Grant National Park was established October 1, 1890, one week after Sequoia National Park, to protect a single grove of giant sequoias. On March 4, 1940, Congress created Kings Canyon National Park, absorbing General Grant into it as Grant Grove.

The merged park has been administered jointly with Sequoia National Park since 1940. NPS gives the combined parks at 865,964 acres, of which Kings Canyon's share is 461,901 acres.

The park spans two districts: Grant Grove, home to the General Grant Tree, and Cedar Grove, reached by road along the South Fork Kings River, where the canyon the park is named for opens out.

Emotional Thesis

A canyon under a sequoia, not a sequoia in a park

The header painting puts a giant sequoia trunk at the frame's edge, looking down over granite walls and a river the color of glacier melt. That is the park's actual layout: Grant Grove's trees on one side, the Kings River canyon opening below on the other.

This was two parks for fifty years. General Grant National Park was one grove, four square miles, built around a single named tree. Kings Canyon absorbed it and added the granite canyon country the grove had been sitting beside all along.

Landscape Highlights

A sequoia grove, a granite canyon, and the river that cut it

01

Grant Grove

Home to the General Grant Tree, 268.1 feet tall with a ground circumference of 107.5 feet, the second-largest tree in the world by trunk volume. It has held the title Nation's Christmas Tree since 1926 and is the country's only living national shrine.

02

Cedar Grove and the Kings River

Roaring River Falls, Muir Rock, and Zumwalt Meadow line the road along the South Fork Kings River in Cedar Grove, the district that gives the park its name.

03

Granite walls

The canyon is cut through Sierra Nevada granite, the same rock that forms Yosemite's cliffs to the north. Panoramic Point looks out over the range from Grant Grove.

Hidden Discoveries

A tree named for a general, and a park that swallowed it whole

01

The Nation's Christmas Tree

The General Grant Tree carries two titles NPS records without a founding date on the page opened for this essay: the Nation's Christmas Tree and the country's only living national shrine. President Coolidge is credited with the first designation in some accounts, not confirmed on the page used here.

02

A grove absorbed

General Grant National Park existed alongside Sequoia National Park for fifty years as its own four-square-mile unit before the 1940 act folded it into the larger Kings Canyon park as Grant Grove.

03

Redwood Mountain

A separate grove, Redwood Mountain, sits within Kings Canyon and is reached by the Redwood Canyon Trail, distinct from Grant Grove's more visited stands.

Wildlife

A marmot on granite, and a park shared with Sequoia

The joint Sequoia and Kings Canyon parks record 321 wildlife species, 300 of them native, across amphibians, birds, fish, mammals, and reptiles. The extra drawing, a yellow-bellied marmot beside a lupine, is the archive's image of the high granite country, not a count.

01

Marmots sun themselves on the same granite slabs the canyon's cliffs are made of, especially at elevation near Cedar Grove's trailheads.

02

The 321-species figure is for the combined parks, not Kings Canyon alone; the two units share an ecosystem and an administration.

03

Black bears are present throughout; food storage rules apply at Cedar Grove's campgrounds as they do at Sequoia's.

Geology

Granite cut by a river, and a grove that predates the park

The canyon is Sierra Nevada granite, shaped by the South Fork Kings River and by glaciers that widened what the river had already begun cutting.

01

The elevation range NPS gives for the joint parks runs from 1,370 feet at the Middle Fork Kaweah in Sequoia to 14,494 feet at Mount Whitney, also in Sequoia; Kings Canyon's own relief sits within that broader range without matching either extreme.

02

Giant sequoias need the specific combination of water and granite-derived soil the Sierra's west slope provides. Redwood Mountain Grove, at 2,558 acres, is named on the joint fact sheet as one of the parks' major groves.

03

Do not borrow a canyon-depth figure for this park. The NPS pages opened for this essay do not state one, and third-party claims about it being deeper than the Grand Canyon were not used.

A canyon road that closes, and a grove that does not

Summer

Cedar Grove is open, generally May through early or mid-November, and the canyon road along the Kings River is the season's main draw.

Fall

Cedar Grove closes for the season as snow approaches. Grant Grove, at lower elevation, stays reachable longer.

Winter

Snow closes the Cedar Grove road. Grant Grove remains open year-round, including winter trails around the sequoias.

Spring

The Kings River runs high with snowmelt before Cedar Grove's road reopens for the season.

Photography

A trunk, a wall, and a river, together

A sequoia alone can read as any tall tree without something to set its width. A canyon wall alone can read as any granite cliff. This park is the two together, with a river to give both of them scale.

01

Scale at the grove

A person or a trail sign beside the General Grant Tree's trunk is what shows its 107.5-foot ground circumference.

02

The river as anchor

The South Fork Kings River running through the canyon floor gives a wide shot somewhere to rest.

03

Not Yosemite Valley

Cliffs of the same granite stand a short drive north at Yosemite. Keep this park's river and sequoias in frame so the two are not interchangeable.

Stewardship / Safety

A joint administration, and rules that follow the season

Kings Canyon shares staff, rules, and a fact sheet with Sequoia National Park next door. What is open changes with snow and fire season.

  1. 01

    Cedar Grove's road and campgrounds are seasonal. Confirm dates with NPS before planning a Kings River trip.

  2. 02

    Store food properly; black bears are active throughout both parks.

  3. 03

    Stay on trail near sequoia root systems. Giant sequoias have shallow, wide roots easily compacted by foot traffic.

  4. 04

    Wildfire and smoke can close areas of either park with little notice. Check current conditions before a visit.

Current conditions, fees, and alerts are on the National Park Service site.
